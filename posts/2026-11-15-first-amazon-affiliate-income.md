Let me save you the suspense: the first Amazon affiliate money my blog earned was not life-changing. It won't pay the mortgage. It barely makes a dent in my first goal of $1,000 a month.

And I still count it as one of the best things that happened to my business this year.

Because first commissions aren't really about the money. They answer the question that keeps most of us from starting at all: does this actually work, or is it one of those things that only works for people with a big audience and a free afternoon every day? I don't have a big audience. I have a toddler and a few hours a week. And the answer, it turns out, is yes, it works. Slowly, honestly, and with a lot more checking than the "passive income" videos admit.

So here's the real story: what earned those first commissions, the rules I'd want every beginner to know, and the four things I'd do faster if I started again tomorrow.

## What actually earned my first commissions

The money came from my home and lifestyle blog, House & Habit. Nothing about it is fancy. It's a simple website, and every post recommends home products from Amazon with my affiliate links.

What makes it work for a busy mom is how the posts get made. I'm not a developer, and I don't sit down to a blank page twice a week. Instead:

1. **A scheduled Claude task drafts a post every Tuesday and Friday**, with the Amazon products and the photo prompts already in it.
2. **I read every draft** and change what doesn't sound right.
3. **I check every single product myself** and swap in my own SiteStripe links. If a product looks flimsy, has worrying reviews or seems likely to come back as a return, it's out.
4. **I make the photos in ChatGPT** from the written prompts.
5. **I publish through GitHub and Netlify**, which sounds more technical than it is.

All of that takes me under two hours on a post day, during my daughter's playtime. That's the whole engine. One blog, two posts a week, real human checks on every link.

If there's one lesson in that list, it's this: the AI did the heavy lifting, but the trust came from step 3. Readers buy from people who'd actually recommend the thing. Which brings me to the boring part that matters more than it sounds.

## The rules nobody reads until they have to

Before you get excited about a first sale, know how Amazon Associates actually treats beginners. These are the ones that catch people out:

- **You need three qualifying sales within 180 days.** Amazon only reviews your application once you've made sales, and it asks for at least three in your first 180 days. Miss it, and you may have to start over.
- **Your own orders don't count.** Buying through your own link to "get things moving" won't help, and it can get you into trouble.
- **You have to say you're an Amazon Associate.** Every page with your links needs a clear disclosure, using the wording Amazon gives in its Operating Agreement.
- **Every place you share links needs to be listed in your account**, including your Pinterest profile if you use it.
- **Reviewers look at your actual site.** A blog with a few thin posts and nothing else is a harder sell than one with real, useful content.

Here's a quick checklist I'd tick off before applying:

- [ ] At least a handful of genuinely helpful posts are live
- [ ] An affiliate disclosure is on every post with links
- [ ] An About page says who you are and what the blog is for
- [ ] You have a way to bring readers in from day one (more on that next)
- [ ] You've put a reminder in your calendar for day 150

That 180-day clock is the real reason I'd change how I started. Here's what I'd speed up.

## Faster thing #1: start Pinterest on day one

I opened the Pinterest account for my blog only recently. If I started again, I'd open it the same day as the blog.

New websites wait a long time for Google to send them anyone. Pinterest works differently: it's a visual search engine, and people use it specifically to find ideas and things to buy, especially for home, gifts, kids and beauty. A pin can start sending visits long before a search engine knows your blog exists. And visits are what turn into those three qualifying sales inside the 180 days.

Pins also take time to warm up. They often need weeks before they're seen much, so every week you wait is a week of growth you don't get back. My long-term goal is a million monthly views, and the honest truth is that the clock on that started the day I opened the account, not the day I launched the blog.

If you want the full setup, I wrote it out in [Pinterest for a brand-new blog: a 30-day plan that doesn't eat your evenings](/pinterest-for-new-blog). Starting Pinterest early is the biggest change. The second one is about what you write.

## Faster thing #2: publish posts people are ready to buy from

A lovely post about the feeling of a cozy home is nice to read. A post called "Cozy Living Room Finds Under $50" is nice to read and easy to buy from. If I were starting from zero, I'd make my first ten posts lean hard toward the second kind:

- **Gift guides**, for a person or a budget
- **"Best of" lists**, like the best throws for a reading corner
- **Problem-solving posts**, where a product is the answer, like organizing a tiny entryway
- **Seasonal posts published early**, so they're live before people start shopping

You can still write the dreamy posts. Just make sure the early ones give readers a clear reason to click.

## Faster thing #3: automate the drafting, but never the checking

If I'd waited until I "had time" to write two posts a week by hand, I'd still be waiting. Setting up the scheduled drafts is what made consistency possible with a toddler at home. I'd set that up sooner.

What I'd never hand over is the final product check. AI can pick something that looks perfect in a listing and turns out to be a returns magnet. So I use a prompt like this to do a first pass, and then I look at every product myself anyway:

```
Here are the Amazon products in my blog post draft: [paste names and links].
For each one, tell me:
1. Does it actually fit the post's topic and reader?
2. What should I check in the reviews before I recommend it
   (quality complaints, sizing, returns, safety)?
3. Is there a reason a careful reader might not trust it?
Don't tell me a product is good unless the listing supports it.
Flag anything you're unsure about.
```

The AI's answer is a starting point, not a verdict. The swap to my own SiteStripe link only happens after I've looked for myself.

## Faster thing #4: set a money target and watch three numbers

My first target is $1,000 a month. Having a number makes the small wins feel like steps instead of crumbs.

Your Associates reports show you plenty, but at the start I'd only watch three things:

| Number | What it tells you | What to do if it's low |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clicks | Are readers interested enough to look? | Make products easier to spot, add buying posts |
| Items ordered | Are clicks turning into purchases? | Pick better-reviewed, more useful products |
| Visits to your blog | Is anyone arriving at all? | More pins, pinned more consistently |

That's it. Don't drown in reports in month one. Watch whether those three are slowly moving up, and fix the lowest one first.

## The honest timeline (and what comes next)

I want to be really clear here, because this is where a lot of affiliate content gets slippery: first commissions don't mean a steady income has arrived. They mean the pipes are connected. Plenty of people take months to see their first sale, and plenty of blogs never get past small amounts. It builds over months of showing up, not overnight, and nobody can promise you a result.

For me, the first commissions are the reason I'm keeping the two posts a week going. I'm putting real effort into Pinterest. And I have more blogs in mind to start, using the same setup, because now I know the setup works.
