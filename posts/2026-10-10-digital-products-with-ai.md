Not long ago, "make a digital product" meant a PDF. You'd write a guide, design it in Canva, export it and hope it looked good on a phone.

Today most of what I sell isn't a PDF at all. It's an interactive page: a reading tracker you can tap through, a dashboard that tells an Etsy seller what to do next, an alphabet game for toddlers, a coloring book you color on screen. I'm not a developer. I build every one of them by describing what I want to AI, testing what comes back, and refining it until it works.

This entry is the exact process I use, in the order I use it.

## What I've built this way

So you know this isn't theory, here's a sample of what has come out of this process for my shop, Creative Digital Story:

- **Guides and courses:** the Interactive Digital Products Guide, the Etsy Success Guide 2026 Edition, and my newest course, Make Money with an Automated Affiliate Blog.
- **Interactive tools:** a reading tracker, an Etsy Next Action dashboard, and the Pinterest Digital Wealth Bundle, a set of six AI-powered Pinterest tools.
- **Just-for-fun products:** a toddler alphabet game and an interactive coloring book.
- **Spreadsheets:** trackers for small business owners, built in Excel.

Every one of these started as a conversation.

## Step 1: Start with a job, not a product

The worst way to start is "I want to sell a planner." The best way is to name a specific job someone is struggling with. "Etsy sellers open their dashboard and don't know what to fix first" is a job. That one became my Etsy Next Action dashboard.

Write the job down in one sentence before you open any AI tool. If you can't, you don't have a product yet.

## Step 2: Let AI interview you

Instead of asking AI to "make a product," I ask it to ask me questions first. Something like:

```
I want to build an interactive tool that helps [who] do [job].
Before you build anything, ask me 8 to 10 questions about
the buyer, what they need to see first, and what would make
this worth paying for.
```

Answering those questions forces you to make the decisions you'd otherwise skip, and the build that follows is far better for it.

## Step 3: Outline before you build

Next I ask for a structure: the sections, screens or modules, and what each one does. This is the cheapest moment to change your mind: moving a section around in an outline takes seconds, while moving it in a finished product means rebuilding. Read the outline as your customer would, and check that it ends with them doing something, not just knowing something.

## Step 4: Build it as one interactive page

This is where it gets fun. I ask Claude to build the product as a single, self-contained web page, and I describe the look in plain words: my brand colors, the fonts, how it should feel on a phone. Then I open it and use it like a customer would.

You don't need to read the code. You need to notice things like:

- Does it look right on a phone?
- Is it obvious what to tap first?
- Does anything break, overlap or feel slow?

Then I describe what's wrong in normal language ("the buttons are too close together on mobile," "the progress bar resets when I refresh") and ask for a fix. Most products take several rounds of this. That's normal.

## Step 5: Test it on someone who isn't you

You already know how your product works, so you're the worst tester. Hand it to a friend, ideally one who matches the buyer, and watch where they hesitate. Every pause is a fix.

## Step 6: Host it and package it

My interactive products live on Netlify, which hosts simple web pages for free. The buyer gets a link, which works on any device and never needs downloading. For spreadsheets and guides, the file itself is the product.

Then comes the packaging, which matters more than people expect: a clear product name, three or four mockup images made in Canva, and a description that leads with the job it does, not the features.

![A styled flat lay of product mockup images printed out and arranged on a desk](/images/digital-products-with-ai-3.jpg)

## Step 7: List it and point people to it

I sell through a Beacons storefront, which handles payment and delivery and doubles as my link in bio. Then the product needs a way to be found: Pinterest pins, Threads posts and, now, entries in this blog.

## The lesson that changed how I work

Here's what nobody tells you about building with AI: **making the product stops being the hard part.** When you can build a polished interactive tool in an afternoon, the bottleneck moves almost entirely to marketing. Who is this for? Where do they hang out? Why would they trust you?

I learned this the slow way, by building product after product while my marketing stayed thin. A shelf full of good products doesn't sell itself. These days I try to split my time the other way around: less building, more getting each product in front of the right people. It's also a big part of why this blog exists.

So if you're starting out, build one product, not five. Then spend the time you saved getting that one product seen.

## A quick checklist before you build

- [ ] I can describe the buyer's problem in one sentence.
- [ ] I've let AI ask me questions before building.
- [ ] I have an outline I'm happy with.
- [ ] I've tested it on a phone, and on someone else.
- [ ] I have a name, mockups and a description that leads with the result.
- [ ] I know where my first 100 visitors will come from.

That last box is the one most people leave empty. Fill it in before you start building.
